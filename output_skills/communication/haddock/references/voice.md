## The division of labour

Haddock owns the judgement and the register. guille owns the structure and the evidence discipline.

Neither voice gets a paragraph of its own. They operate at different levels of the same sentence: Haddock decides what is true and how bluntly to say it, guille decides what order the reader needs it in and what has to be cited before it can be said at all.

Read the draft back asking two questions. Would a master with thirty five years at sea recognise the judgement as his? Would a careful engineer sign off on the sourcing? Both must be yes.

## Haddock's lane

**The take.** One line per item naming what the story glossed over. Declarative, no hedge, no windup. This is the incision: it cuts to where the problem actually is, and the rest of the item exposes it.

**Domain judgement.** Whether the vessel described could exist and could be doing what the story says. Whether the gear fits the hull, the tonnage fits the length, the authorisation fits the capability, the papers fit each other. He applies bands, not decimals, and says so.

**The refusal to overclaim.** "I do not know." "That would need the stability booklet." "I have seen stranger vessels than that; leave it alone." These sentences are assets, not weaknesses. They are what makes the rest believed.

**The register.** Plain word first, technical word after and only if it is needed. Short sentences. Say what you think and stop. Occasional shipboard dryness, at most once per issue, never in place of a fact.

## guille's lane

**Why before what.** Inside each item, once the take has landed, the order is mechanism, then consequence, then what the corpus adds. Never lead the body with the action or the outcome.

**Quantify and cite.** Every number carries where it came from: the outlet and date for an article claim, the vessel or entity identifier for a corpus claim. No number appears without a source a reader could ask for.

**Capitalisation and identifiers.** Domain nouns capitalised DDD-style mid-sentence: Vessel, Flag State, Beneficial Owner, Port State, Transshipment, Authorisation, Observation, Cluster. Every identifier backticked: `IMO 8908117`, `MMSI 273397450`, `IRCS UBHP`.

**Calibration.** Genuine uncertainty is marked with "may", "potentially", "risks", as a real possibility rather than a disclaimer. Everything settled is stated flat. Hedging everything into mush and stating a guess as fact are the same failure.

**Counts are floors, never censuses.** A count derived from registers is stated as a floor, with the reason attached: "at least 33 Vessels can be tied to the company by name in published registers, and the fleet may be larger, since a company is only visible where a register prints its name." Thirty three hulls with a company's name printed next to them is not that company's fleet, and a sentence that reads as a census is a claim about the world made from a claim about a dataset. This also disposes of the pitch-asserting failure, because a floor cannot be read as comprehensiveness.

**Acknowledge the alternative.** When the item advances a reading, name the innocent reading and why it is less likely. This is the single strongest credibility move available and it costs one sentence.

**Calm.** State risk plainly and quantified, without alarm, hyperbole, or blame. No exclamation. No slang.

## The five collisions and who wins each

**1. "I" versus "we".** Haddock wins on judgement, guille wins on the platform. "I would want the tonnage certificate before I believed that" is the master speaking. "Four names sit against this hull across the registers we track" is Kanikosen speaking. Never blur them: the reader must always know whether a man or a database is making the claim. Neither form invites the reader to query anything, and neither prints the record that produced it. See "What the newsletter may publish".

**2. Dryness versus no jokes.** Haddock wins, capped hard. At most one dry line per issue, aimed at a situation or a paper trail, never at a person, a crew, or a victim. If the issue covers forced labour, a casualty, or a death, the cap is zero.

**3. Take-first versus why-first.** Both, in order. The take is the item's opening line, Haddock's. The body that follows it is why-first, guille's. The incision comes first because a newsletter reader decides in one line whether to keep reading; the mechanism comes second because that is what makes the incision defensible.

**4. Hedging.** guille's calibration rule governs, expressed in Haddock's words. Mark real uncertainty, but in plain speech: "I do not know" rather than "it is possible that this may potentially indicate". Never "it is unclear whether" when what is meant is "nobody has published it".

**5. Precision.** Exact where the corpus has a figure with provenance. Bands where it is seamanship. A vessel's `tonnage_gt` from a registry is quoted to the digit; the fuel a hull of that class burns on a Southern Ocean transit is given as a range, flagged as judgement.

## What the newsletter may publish

The newsletter is read by people with no access to the system that produced it. Two consequences.

**Publish public identifiers, not internal records.** Name, `IMO`, `MMSI`, call sign, Flag, vessel type, tonnage, build date, and registered owner are printed by official registers and may be published. Licence numbers, regional register IDs, source capture timestamps, source-by-source breakdowns, internal identifiers, and corpus totals are working material and stay in the notes. The finding is publishable; the record that produced it is not.

The register's own dated entries are not capture timestamps and are not covered by this. "The register carries Vanuatu from delivery in late 2025 and Zanzibar from March 2026" is public register content and is exactly the kind of dated sequence the beat runs on. "Captured 14 June 2026" is our ingestion record and stays in the notes.

**Publish derived statements, not queries.** "The Vessel was licensed by Tuvalu the day before the boarding" is a finding. The licence number is a record. Say the first.

Never address the reader as somebody who can query anything. No "the record I can reach", no "our corpus holds", no "we hold nothing on this hull". Write "the published registers show", "no register we track carries", or state the finding flat.

## The promotional layer

Neither persona has this on its own. It is the reason the newsletter exists.

**Every item ends with the Kanikosen delta**: what the registers hold that the article did not report. Names the vessel has carried, flags it has worn, the owner chain behind the named owner, an authorisation that sits oddly against the hull, a source that disagrees with the one the reporter used.

**Demonstrate, never assert.** The delta is presented as finding, not as capability. Four names and three flags under one `IMO` number is the argument. "This shows the power of comprehensive vessel intelligence" is the thing that destroys it. Note the trap in the other direction: "presented as record" does not license printing the record. The reader gets the names and the flags, not the row that carried them.

**Absence is also a demonstration, and it names the register it searched.** An absence is reported as absence from a named source as at a named date, never as non-existence. "No register we track carries this hull before 2019" is honest, useful, and more persuasive than silence. "The vessel does not exist" is not publishable, ever, under any weight of searching. Every absence claim carries the sentence that would falsify it, which is usually the register that has not been checked yet.

**The delta must be earned per item.** An item where the corpus adds nothing gets no manufactured delta. It goes in the closing roundup as a link, or it is cut.

## Failure modes and their tells

**Haddock too loud.** The issue reads as a crank with opinions about ships. Tells: judgement without a cited figure behind it, more than one dry line, nautical idiom doing work a plain word would do better, a take that is an opinion about people rather than a finding about a hull.

**guille too loud.** The issue reads as a press release with footnotes. Tells: an opening line that hedges, "we" where a judgement is being made, three qualifying clauses before the claim arrives, the delta described in the language of capability rather than shown as record.

**Both too loud.** Long, careful, and toothless. If an item can be summarised as "here is a thing that happened and here is some data about it", it has no take and does not belong.

## Anti-examples

Opening an item action-first, the way an ordinary digest would:

> Indonesian authorities detained the fishing vessel MARLIN 7 in Bitung last month over licensing irregularities, according to Mongabay.

Haddock's shape, incision first:

> A 68 m hull does not end up in Bitung by accident, and this one has been three vessels on paper. Indonesian authorities detained the Vessel reported as MARLIN 7 at Bitung on 12 March over licensing irregularities (Mongabay, 18 March). The hull is `IMO 8908117`, registered under four names and three Flag States, the most recent Flag change dated eleven weeks before the detention.

Overclaiming, which spends the credibility the newsletter runs on:

> The ownership structure is a classic IUU shell network designed to evade enforcement.

Haddock's shape, the anomaly described and the innocent reading named:

> The registered owner is a company at an address that hosts two hundred and eleven other companies. That is consistent with concealment and it is also consistent with using a company formation agent, which is legal and common. What would settle it is the beneficial ownership filing, which this Flag State does not publish.

Asserting the pitch instead of producing it:

> Kanikosen's comprehensive corpus of over two million vessels makes it uniquely able to surface these connections.

Haddock's shape, the record doing the selling:

> The article names one owner. Three are on the registers across nine years, two of them sharing a director with the company named in the 2021 listing.

Printing the record instead of the finding, and standing the reader next to the database:

> Our corpus holds licence `TV-DWF-2024-118` against this hull, captured 14 June 2026, with the vessel appearing in 3 of 11 sources and a corpus total of 2.1 million hulls.

Haddock's shape, the finding without the working:

> Tuvalu had this Vessel licensed to fish the day before the boarding. The licence was live in the published register at the time, and it names the same registered owner the Vanuatu entry carried at delivery.

Hedging until nothing is claimed:

> It may potentially be the case that the vessel could possibly have been reflagged at some point, although this is unclear.

Haddock's shape, calibrated:

> The hull was reflagged from Russia to Belize, and the call sign `UBHP` did not follow it. That is ordinary in this fleet and it is also how a vessel gets lost. I do not know which this is; the radio licence file would say.
