These are working heuristics, not rules. Apply them as bands. The question is whether a claim sits inside the physically sensible envelope for that class of vessel, and where it does not, what the cheapest explanation is.

## What the pass is for

Reporters get vessel details from press releases, court filings, and registry screenshots, and those details are frequently wrong in specific, recognisable ways. The pass separates three things: the reporter transcribed something badly, the source itself is wrong, or the vessel is genuinely anomalous and the story is under-reported rather than over-reported.

Confusing the first two with the third is the worst error available here, in both directions. Calling a typo a conspiracy destroys credibility. Calling a real deception a typo wastes the one thing the corpus is for.

Prefer the cheapest explanation, in this order: unit error, transposed field, decimal point, imperial reading, net tonnage for gross, horsepower for kilowatt, neighbouring record bleeding across. Exhaust those before reaching for anything interesting.

## Build, yard, and era

Material against date. Steel throughout the modern era. Wood common in older and smaller hulls. Fibreglass essentially not before the late 1950s and never at large sizes. Aluminium in narrow niches.

Technology against date. Refrigerated seawater tanks, brine freezing to ultra low temperature, ramp sterns and factory decks, continuous pumping trawl systems: each has an era before which it did not exist on this kind of hull. An old hull with modern plant is a conversion, which is worth saying, or a bad merge, which is worth checking.

Sister ships. Vessels from one yard and build run share dimensions, tonnage, and machinery almost exactly. Where a batch of near identical hulls appears and one member diverges on a single attribute, that member is very probably the error. This works even where nothing can be verified externally, and the corpus makes it cheap to check.

Yard capability. A yard that has only ever produced hulls under 30 m did not produce a 90 m factory trawler. Yard identity anchors a claim, it is not decoration.

Legitimate change. Lengthening, re-engining, conversion between fisheries, and change of gear are normal across a long life. Dimensions and tonnage should change over time in those cases. Look for the conversion story before calling a temporal change an error.

## Type, gear, target, ground

Gear against hull. A purse seiner carries a power block, a skiff, and a net drum, not trawl doors. A longliner carries line haulers and a shooting chute. A jigger carries jig machines and lights. Trawl gear on a hull that plainly cannot tow is an error or a genuine multipurpose vessel, and the two are distinguishable.

Gear against target. Krill, toothfish, tuna, squid, and demersal whitefish each have a small set of gears that actually take them. A mismatch is informative.

Type against size. A 12 m distant water trawler does not exist. A 150 m artisanal vessel does not exist.

Ground against capability. A vessel authorised in a Southern Ocean fishery must be able to work there: ice capability, or at minimum the size, endurance, and freezing capacity. An authorisation to fish a ground the hull could not survive is a strong signal, and usually a clustering error before it is a scandal.

Support vessels miscoded as catchers. Reefers, carriers, bunkers, and tenders logged as fishing vessels is a high value error class, because every transshipment claim depends on getting it right. The tells are no fishing gear, disproportionate hold capacity, and the wrong deck arrangement.

## Identity and papers

IMO number. Seven digits with a check digit; verify the checksum before quoting one. IMO numbers reached fishing vessels late and mainly the larger ones, so absence on a small hull is normal and never reported as a gap. Presence on a hull too small or too old to have plausibly been issued one is worth a look.

Validate an unfamiliar number before doubting it. Anyone used to the 7 and 9 series reads `IMO 1120754` as a typo. It is not: the check digit validates, and the 1 series is current issue for recently registered hulls. Run the checksum before treating a number as suspect, and expect the 1 series on anything delivered in the last few years.

MMSI. Nine digits, the first three identifying the flag state. A mismatch against the recorded flag is a genuine finding and not automatically an error: it commonly means a sale or reflagging without the radio licence being reissued, which is exactly what this beat exists to see. Never silently reconcile one to the other.

Call signs and registry numbers. Each flag has its conventions, including the EU fleet register format. An identifier whose shape does not fit the claimed flag is worth naming.

Flag against port of registry. They should cohere. Where they do not, it is a data error or a reflagging in progress.

Names. Transliteration variation across Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Russian, and Spanish sources is normal and is not an error. Name recycling across unrelated hulls is common, especially in numbered series, and is the single most dangerous thing to reason from.

Identify by event chronology, not by name. Where a story describes a sequence, a reflagging, a sale, a detention, a name change, match the sequence against the registers' dated entries rather than matching the name. A name match is one weak signal. A dated sequence match across two or three fields, say Vanuatu from delivery in late 2025 and Zanzibar from March 2026 against reporting that says Vanuatu at the time of the offence and struck off after, is an identification. Say in the item which fields carried it, because that is what makes it checkable.

Sequential fleet naming. Numbered series from one operator are a real and useful pattern for cross validation, and a trap for automatic merging.

## Lifecycle and time

A build year later than the first observation of the vessel is impossible.

Activity after a loss, scrapping, or deletion date is a stale source, a reused identity, or a stolen one. Say which is most likely and why.

Rapid successive flag and name changes are real behaviour with meaning. Frequency and sequence carry more information than any single change.

A registered owner change that arrives alongside a flag change is routine and says nothing about control. Reflagging usually requires an owning entity in or acceptable to the new register, so the owner name changes as paperwork, not as a sale. Say so in the item, once, and name the beneficial ownership filing as the thing that would settle it.

A vessel with one name, one flag, and one owner since delivery is a finding in this beat, not a null result. It takes identity off the table and leaves whatever else the story is about, usually the licensing, standing on its own. Worth one sentence, because most hulls here cannot say the same.

## Ownership and address

An ownership chain terminating at an address known for company formation services is a finding, and it is also ordinary commercial practice. Both readings go in the item. What separates them is the count of unrelated companies at that address, the jurisdictions involved, and whether the same directors recur across the chain.

Beneficial ownership is not inferred from structure. The corpus links registered owners and operators; who ultimately benefits is a different method and a different team, and the newsletter says so rather than guessing.

## Where the takes usually are

The article names one owner and the registers carry three across the vessel's life.

The article treats a vessel as new to the fleet and the corpus has it under a different name and flag five years earlier.

The article reports a detention and the corpus shows the flag change that preceded it by weeks.

Two authoritative sources disagree about the same hull on the same date, and the disagreement is the story rather than a defect.

Two or three live hulls carry the name in the story, and an outlet has attached one hull's flag or tonnage to another. The collision is usually a better finding than anything about the vessel itself.

An authorisation exists that the hull could not physically exercise, which is usually a clustering error and occasionally is not.

A vessel is described as a catcher and the arrangement says carrier, which changes what a reported transshipment means.

## What is rare but real

Odd vessels exist. Converted hulls, one-off yard builds, genuinely multipurpose vessels, and vessels with implausible-looking but correct papers are all common enough to expect. "Unusual, and real" is a conclusion to reach often and without embarrassment, and an item that reaches it honestly is worth more than one that manufactures a scandal.
